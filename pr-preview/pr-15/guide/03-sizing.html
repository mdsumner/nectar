3. Sizing

Status: draft

Two questions: how big is the machine you have now, and how big does the new one need to be? Then turn the answer into Service Units for your request.

How big is the machine I’m on?

From inside the machine

On a Linux machine (or a Mac, for some of these), open a terminal and type:

nproc                                          # number of CPU cores
free -h                                        # RAM ("total" column)
df -h                                          # disk size and usage per filesystem
lsblk                                          # disks and volumes attached
cat /proc/cpuinfo | grep 'model name' | head -1   # CPU model

If the machine is a Nectar VM, it can tell you about itself through the OpenStack metadata service (OpenStack docs):

# the instance's name, availability zone and project ID
curl -s http://169.254.169.254/openstack/latest/meta_data.json

# the flavour name, e.g. m3.large (EC2-style metadata)
curl -s http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/instance-type

meta_data.json does not include the flavour; the instance-type path should. TODO(verify) both on a current Nectar VM.

From the Nectar dashboard

  • Project > Compute > Overview: pie charts of how much of your project’s quota (instances, vCPU, RAM, volumes) is in use.
  • Project > Compute > Instances: each instance’s flavour, IP address and availability zone. Click an instance’s name for details.
  • Usage menu: Service Units used over time, and how many are left (usage graphs).

Reading flavour names

A flavour name is family.size. The general-purpose families (flavours, Aug 2026):

Family For RAM per vCPU Root disk Sizes
t3 Tiny, light jobs 1 GB 10 GB 1–4 vCPU
m3 Balanced, the default 2 GB 30 GB m3.xsmall 1 vCPU/2 GB to m3.xxlarge 32/64
r3 RAM-heavy work 4 GB 30 GB up to 32 vCPU/128 GB
c3 CPU priority 2 GB 30 GB up to 64 vCPU/128 GB
p3 Preemptible: ¼ of the SU, but can be killed within 24 h 2 GB 30 GB up to 32 vCPU/64 GB

The sizes go xsmall, small, medium, large, xlarge, xxlarge, each doubling the cores and RAM of the one before (e.g. m3.medium = 4 vCPU/8 GB, m3.large = 8/16).

Other things to know:

  • No ephemeral disk on these standard flavours: you get only the root disk (30 GB, or 10 GB on t3). Data goes on a volume (05-storage.md). Only the reserved GPU (g1, g2, g3) and huge-RAM (h4) flavours come with ephemeral disk.
  • Node-private flavours exist at some nodes, e.g. tas.c16m64 at Tasmania (16 vCPU, 64 GB RAM). They’re arranged with the node by support ticket, and their naming and SU rates aren’t documented centrally: TODO(verify) with the node.
  • m1 and m2 flavours are deprecated; if you see them on an old VM, plan to move to m3.
  • GPU (g1/g2/g3) and huge-RAM (h4) flavours are reserved and need a special request.

How big does mine need to be?

Rules of thumb:

  • RAM is usually the limit. Take the largest data you hold in memory at once, and allow 2–3× for copies made during processing, plus a few GB for the operating system and RStudio. Watch free -h (or htop) on your laptop while your biggest job runs.
  • Cores only help if your work runs in parallel (e.g. parallel, future, furrr in R; multiprocessing, dask in Python; or several jobs at once). A single-threaded R script runs no faster on 16 cores than on 2.
  • Disk: the root disk is 30 GB and is lost when the instance is deleted. Put data on a volume sized to your data plus working space, or in object storage.
  • Start small. You can resize an instance to a bigger flavour later (it reboots), so start with the smallest flavour that fits and grow.
  • Matching shape to family: lots of RAM per core → r3; lots of cores and modest RAM → m3 or c3; disposable batch work that can restart → p3.

Back-of-envelope Service Units

Each flavour costs a fixed number of SU per hour for as long as the instance exists, running or shut off (Service Units). The arithmetic is:

SU = SU per hour × hours × number of instances

(a year is about 8,760 hours; a month about 730)

SU per hour for some common flavours (flavours, Aug 2026):

Flavour vCPU / RAM SU/hour SU/year (always on)
m3.small 2 / 4 GB 0.057 500
m3.medium 4 / 8 GB 0.114 1,000
m3.large 8 / 16 GB 0.228 2,000
m3.xlarge 16 / 32 GB 0.457 4,000
m3.xxlarge 32 / 64 GB 0.913 8,000
r3.large 8 / 32 GB 0.417 3,654
r3.xlarge 16 / 64 GB 0.834 7,308
c3.xlarge 16 / 32 GB 0.694 6,077
p3.xlarge 12 / 24 GB 0.086 750

Within m3, SU scale exactly with cores: about 250 SU per vCPU per year. r3 costs about 1.8× and c3 about 1.5× the m3 rate for the same core count.

Worked examples

You want Sum SU Bundle
One m3.small all year 0.057 × 8,760 ≈ 500 Standard (4,000) with lots to spare
One 16-core m3.xlarge all year 0.457 × 8,760 ≈ 4,000 All of Standard, nothing left over: ask for Powerful (8,000)
One 16-core, 64 GB r3.xlarge all year 0.834 × 8,760 ≈ 7,300 Powerful (8,000)
Two m3.large for 6 months 2 × 0.228 × 4,380 ≈ 2,000 Standard
One m3.xxlarge for 3 months of heavy work, then an m3.medium for 9 months 0.913 × 2,190 + 0.114 × 6,570 ≈ 2,000 + 750 = 2,750 Standard

Leave some headroom (say 20%): reaching 100% of your SU expires the allocation (see the expiry table in 02-your-allocation.md). Also check the bundle’s other limits: Standard caps you at 64 vCPU and 128 GB RAM across all instances at once (bundle contents).

Shut off is not off

A shut-off instance still uses SU at the full rate. To stop the meter, shelve it (the instance is saved and can be unshelved later, keeping its disk) or delete it. Unused SU don’t carry over to the next year, but SU burned by an idle machine are gone for good. See 08-looking-after-it.md.
